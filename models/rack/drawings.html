Systainer3 S76 modular rack

A 6-bay wall rack in 14 printed pieces, split down the middle because a 265 mm case is wider than the X2D's 256 mm plate. No bolts and no inserts: the courses interlock, and the centre seam is a glued V joint.

Piece sections

at the centre seam · scale ≈ 1.3:1

The boss. The wall swells from 3.2 to 9.6 mm over the top and bottom 10 mm — sized so 1.65 mm of wall survives beside the socket. A 3.2 mm wall is too thin to hold a dovetail that carries tension, and the swell goes outward so it costs no bay width.

Lips. Only a plate that is a floor carries them. The top cap is a ceiling, so it has none.

SHEET 3

Shelf plan — the centre seam

one course · scale ≈ 1.2:1

How it goes together. Butter the V, press the halves together sideways, clamp until the glue grabs. No undercut, so no assembly direction to get wrong.

Glue carries the tension that the seam sees at mid-span, where a plain butt joint would act as a hinge and let the shelf sag into a V. The joint adds 60.08% more bonded area than a flat butt, and it locates the halves so they cannot slide vertically past each other while the glue sets.

It is why nothing needs support. A V is constant along the depth, which is the print axis — no staircase, no overhang, and no bands at all. The flared tabs it replaced cost ~250 breakpoints a piece and still left one flank overhanging.

Every opening is peaked. The shelf stands as a vertical wall in the print, so a flat-topped hole is a horizontal roof — the first version filled all of them with tree supports. The peak insets one layer per layer, which slices as a true 45°. The flat bottom needs nothing: material ending as the print rises is free.

Why not a diamond. Same idea, but a rotated square is always half its bounding box however you size it. Peaking only the top keeps 80% of the hole instead of 50%.

The seam frame is 14.15 mm — wider than the 12 mm frame elsewhere, because the groove is cut 2.15 mm into it.

SHEET 7

French cleat

section through the top of the rack

No bracket to attach. The hook is the top cap's own back, trimmed by the bearing plane. Nothing to fasten, and nothing to come loose.

It prints without support for the same reason it works: the hook's underside is the bearing plane, so going up the print it rises and material only ever ends. The strip prints outer-face down, losing height the same way — and its screw holes come out vertical.

This is what ties the two towers. Both seat on the same strip, which is the one thing that stops a course's halves lifting apart — the single direction the seam leaves free. The two strip halves peg together so they cannot be mounted at different heights.

Bottom cap carries a full-section pad instead of a hook, holding the rack parallel to the wall. Full-section on purpose: clipping it meant the rest of the cap reappeared in mid-air at the back face, and a slicer supported it from the bed.

2.05 kg of filament — the shelves are already cut back

Skeletonising the shelves to a frame, ribs and 36 openings a course takes roughly a quarter off the whole rack. What is left is sized by the print, not by eye: each opening is peaked at 8 mm so nothing ever has to bridge across its top.

Shelves: skeletonised ✓

A 12 mm frame, 6 mm ribs and 12.97 × 48.67 mm openings. The case bears near its edges, over the frame, so the middle was the cheapest material to lose.
